Overview

Three lenses on the same blood gas

There are three widely taught ways of analysing acid–base disturbances. They are complementary, not competing: they use the same chemistry and, applied properly, reach the same diagnosis. They differ in which variables they treat as causes and which they treat as markers.

1. Traditional ("physiological" / Boston)

Henderson–Hasselbalch, bicarbonate as the metabolic marker, the anion gap, the delta ratio and empirical compensation rules. Quick and familiar at the bedside.

Go to section →

2. Base excess (Copenhagen)

The standard base excess quantifies the metabolic component independently of PCO₂. It can be partitioned into sodium–chloride, albumin and unmeasured-anion effects.

Go to section →

3. Physicochemical (Stewart)

pH is set by three independent variables: PCO₂, the strong ion difference (SID) and total weak acid (ATOT). This explains fluid-induced acidosis and hypoalbuminaemic alkalosis.

Approach 1

Traditional (physiological / Boston) approach

Henderson–Hasselbalch

pH = 6.1 + log₁₀ ( [HCO₃⁻] ÷ (0.23 × PaCO₂ in kPa) ) = 6.1 + log₁₀ ( [HCO₃⁻] ÷ (0.03 × PaCO₂ in mmHg) ) 1 kPa = 7.50 mmHg · 6.1 = pKa′ of the CO₂/HCO₃⁻ system in plasma at 37 °C

The equation shows that pH depends on the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO₂. Use it to check internal consistency: if the HCO₃⁻ calculated from pH and PCO₂ differs markedly from the reported value, suspect a transcription error or non-simultaneous samples. (Analysers calculate HCO₃⁻ from measured pH and PCO₂, whereas the laboratory "bicarbonate" is usually measured total CO₂, which runs about 1–2 mmol/L higher.)

A step-wise interpretation

  1. History and context first. What do you expect? (e.g. vomiting, diabetes, overdose, COPD.)
  2. Acidaemia or alkalaemia? pH < 7.35 or > 7.45. A normal pH does not exclude a disorder (mixed disorders can cancel out).
  3. Primary process. Which of PaCO₂ and HCO₃⁻ moves in the direction that explains the pH?
  4. Compensation. Is the other variable where the rules predict? If not, there is a second primary disorder.
  5. Anion gap (always, and correct it for albumin) — a raised gap means a high anion gap metabolic acidosis (HAGMA) even when the pH looks normal.
  6. Delta ratio in a HAGMA, to look for a coexisting normal-gap acidosis or metabolic alkalosis.
  7. Osmolar gap in an unexplained HAGMA (toxic alcohols).
  8. Reconcile with the clinical picture and other tests (lactate, ketones, renal function, urinary electrolytes).

Anion gap (AG)

AG = [Na⁺] − ([Cl⁻] + [HCO₃⁻]) typical ≈ 8–12 mmol/L (analyser-dependent) AG with K⁺ = ([Na⁺] + [K⁺]) − ([Cl⁻] + [HCO₃⁻]) ≈ 4 mmol/L higher than the AG without K⁺

The AG is not a real gap — plasma is electroneutral. It represents anions that are not routinely measured: mainly the negative charge on albumin, plus phosphate, sulphate and organic anions, minus unmeasured cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺). Whether K⁺ is included is a matter of local convention; be consistent with your laboratory's reference range.

Correcting for albumin (Figge et al.)

Corrected AG = AG + 0.25 × ( normal albumin − measured albumin ) [albumin in g/L] This site uses normal albumin = 40 g/L: corrected AG = AG + 0.25 × (40 − albumin)

Figge and colleagues found that each 1 g/L fall in albumin lowers the observed AG by about 0.25 mEq/L (2.5 per 10 g/L). Hypoalbuminaemia is very common in acutely unwell patients and can hide a significant HAGMA. Figge et al. expressed the correction relative to "normal albumin"; sources use reference values between 40 and 44 g/L, which changes the corrected AG by up to 1 mmol/L.

Causes

High AG metabolic acidosis (HAGMA)
  • Lactate (shock, sepsis, ischaemia, metformin, seizures)
  • Ketones (diabetic, alcoholic, starvation)
  • Renal failure (sulphate, phosphate, urate and other anions)
  • Toxins: methanol, ethylene glycol, salicylate, paracetamol (pyroglutamic acid / 5-oxoproline), iron, cyanide, others
Normal AG (hyperchloraemic) metabolic acidosis (NAGMA)
  • Chloride load: large volumes of 0.9% saline
  • Gastrointestinal bicarbonate loss: diarrhoea, ileostomy, fistulae, ureteric diversion
  • Renal tubular acidosis; carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide)
  • Recovery phase of DKA (ketoanions lost in urine)

Delta ratio (Δ/Δ) and delta gap

Delta ratio = (AG − normal AG) ÷ (normal HCO₃⁻ − measured HCO₃⁻) = (AG − 12) ÷ (24 − HCO₃⁻) Delta gap = (AG − 12) − (24 − HCO₃⁻) (same information expressed as a difference)

In a pure HAGMA, each mmol/L of acid anion added should lower HCO₃⁻ by roughly one mmol/L, so the ratio should be about 1–2. Use the albumin-corrected AG.

Caveats. These bands are rules of thumb and sources quote slightly different cut-offs (between 0.8 and 1 there is a grey zone). The ratio inherits the wide normal range of the AG and of HCO₃⁻, it is unreliable when the change in HCO₃⁻ is small, and non-bicarbonate buffering and renal handling of anions differ between acids (Rastegar 2007). Treat it as a prompt to look for a second process, not as proof.

Expected compensation

Compensation returns pH towards normal but rarely to normal (chronic respiratory alkalosis is the usual exception). A value outside the predicted range implies a second primary disorder. Osmolar gap

Calculated osmolality (UK units, mmol/L) = 2 × [Na⁺] + [glucose] + [urea] (+ [ethanol] if known) Osmolar gap = measured osmolality − calculated osmolality
  • Measured osmolality must be by freezing-point depression; vapour-pressure osmometry misses volatile alcohols.
  • A gap above about 10 mOsm/kg is commonly treated as raised, but the normal spread is wide: one study found a mean of −2 ± 6 mOsm and showed that absolute values depend heavily on the equation used (Hoffman et al. 1993).
  • Causes of a raised gap: toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), ethanol, isopropanol, mannitol, glycine, propylene glycol; modest rises also occur in DKA, alcoholic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis and renal failure.
  • Timing matters: early after toxic alcohol ingestion the osmolar gap is high and the AG normal; as the parent alcohol is metabolised to toxic acids the osmolar gap falls and the AG rises. A normal osmolar gap therefore does not exclude toxic alcohol poisoning (Kraut & Kurtz 2008; Lynd et al. 2008).
Approach 2

Base excess (Copenhagen) approach

Base excess is the amount of strong acid (or base, if negative) needed to titrate a sample back to pH 7.40 at PCO₂ 5.3 kPa (40 mmHg) and 37 °C. Because it is defined at a standard PCO₂, it isolates the metabolic component. The standard base excess (SBE) models the whole extracellular fluid (blood diluted by interstitial fluid, effective haemoglobin about 3 g/dL), so it is less affected by acute PCO₂ changes than whole-blood base excess.

Standard base excess (Van Slyke equation, a widely used form): SBE = 0.93 × ( [HCO₃⁻] − 24.4 + 14.83 × (pH − 7.40) ) mmol/L

Siggaard-Andersen 1977. Analyser manufacturers implement slightly different versions, so the SBE on your blood gas may differ by a fraction of a mmol/L from this calculation.

SBE tells you how much metabolic acidosis or alkalosis is present, but not why. Partitioning it does that.

Partitioned base excess Simplified

Story, Morimatsu and Bellomo (2004) proposed a simplified bedside version of the Fencl–Stewart approach, building on earlier work by Gilfix et al. (1993). It splits the SBE into the parts explained by sodium and chloride, by albumin, and a remainder due to unmeasured ions (including lactate).

Sodium–chloride effect = [Na⁺] − [Cl⁻] − 38 (mEq/L) Albumin effect = 0.25 × (42 − [albumin g/L]) (mEq/L) Unmeasured ion effect = SBE − (Na–Cl effect) − (albumin effect)
  • Negative values are acidifying; positive values are alkalinising.
  • Na–Cl effect: a low Na⁺ − Cl⁻ difference (hyperchloraemia or hyponatraemia relative to chloride) is acidifying, e.g. after 0.9% saline.
  • Albumin effect: hypoalbuminaemia is alkalinising — a fall of 10 g/L contributes about +2.5 mEq/L.
  • Unmeasured ion effect: a strongly negative value suggests unmeasured anions (lactate, ketones, uraemic anions, toxins). Compare it with the measured lactate: if it is much more negative than the lactate explains, look for other anions.
This is a simplified method. Story et al. used a reference Na–Cl difference of 38 and albumin of 42 g/L; Gilfix and Fencl used fuller versions that also correct chloride for free-water changes and include phosphate. Different versions give somewhat different numbers, and the AG correction above uses 40 g/L rather than 42 g/L. Use partitioning to understand mechanisms rather than as precise quantities.

Worked example: saline-associated acidosis

Na 141, Cl 116, HCO₃⁻ 17, pH 7.33, albumin 34 g/L:

SBE = 0.93 × (17 − 24.4 + 14.83 × (7.33 − 7.40)) ≈ −7.8 Na–Cl effect = 141 − 116 − 38 = −13 (acidifying) Albumin effect = 0.25 × (42 − 34) = +2 (alkalinising) Unmeasured ions = −7.8 − (−13) − 2 ≈ +3 → no unmeasured anion acidosis

The whole base deficit is explained by hyperchloraemia, partly offset by low albumin.

Approach 3

Stewart physicochemical approach

Peter Stewart (1981, 1983) applied three physical laws to plasma: electroneutrality, conservation of mass and the dissociation equilibria of water, the weak acids and CO₂. Solving these simultaneously shows that [H⁺] (and therefore pH) is determined by only three independent variables:

PaCO₂
Set by ventilation relative to CO₂ production.
Strong ion difference (SID)
Strong ions are fully dissociated at physiological pH: Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, lactate⁻ and others. A lower SID is acidifying; a higher SID is alkalinising.
ATOT (total weak acid)
Mainly albumin and phosphate, present as dissociated (A⁻) and undissociated (AH) forms. A higher ATOT is acidifying; hypoalbuminaemia is alkalinising.

Everything else — [H⁺], [OH⁻], [HCO₃⁻], [CO₃²⁻], [A⁻] and [AH] — is a dependent variable. In this framework bicarbonate does not cause anything: it changes because SID, ATOT or PCO₂ change. Water dissociation is the effectively limitless source of H⁺, so "adding or removing H⁺" is not the mechanism; changing the independent variables is.

Apparent SID, effective SID and the strong ion gap

SIDa (apparent) = [Na⁺] + [K⁺] + 2×[Ca²⁺] + 2×[Mg²⁺] − [Cl⁻] − [lactate⁻] (mEq/L) Ca²⁺ = ionised calcium; Mg often total magnesium (ionised Mg is rarely available) Normal often quoted ≈ 40–42 mEq/L SIDe (effective, Figge–Fencl) = [HCO₃⁻] + [A⁻] [Alb⁻] = albumin (g/L) × (0.123 × pH − 0.631) [Pi⁻] = phosphate (mmol/L) × (0.309 × pH − 0.469) SIDe = [HCO₃⁻] + [Alb⁻] + [Pi⁻] SIG (strong ion gap) = SIDa − SIDe

SIDa is what the measured strong ions say the SID should be; SIDe is the negative charge actually carried by the buffer base (bicarbonate plus weak acids). If they differ, there must be unmeasured strong anions (or cations). Unlike the AG, the SIG already accounts for albumin, phosphate and lactate, so a raised SIG points to other unmeasured anions: ketoanions, uraemic anions, toxic-alcohol metabolites, salicylate-related organic acids and so on (Kellum et al. 1995; Kellum 2000).

What is a "normal" SIG? In health the SIG is small, but published reference values differ considerably between studies — from close to zero to several mEq/L — because analysers differ and because small measurement errors in Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are amplified. With the default assumptions on this site (ionised Ca 1.2, Mg 0.9, phosphate 1.0 mmol/L) a "textbook normal" set of results gives a SIG of a few mEq/L. Trends, and large values, are more informative than a single small positive value.

Worked example (DKA case below)

Na 132, K 5.6, Cl 96, lactate 2.0, HCO₃⁻ 8, pH 7.23, albumin 42 g/L; assumed ionised Ca 1.2, Mg 0.9, phosphate 1.0:

SIDa = 132 + 5.6 + 2×1.2 + 2×0.9 − 96 − 2.0 = 43.8 mEq/L Alb⁻ = 42 × (0.123 × 7.23 − 0.631) = 10.8 Pi⁻ = 1.0 × (0.309 × 7.23 − 0.469) = 1.8 SIDe = 8 + 10.8 + 1.8 = 20.6 mEq/L SIG = 43.8 − 20.6 ≈ 23 mEq/L → large unmeasured anion load (ketoanions)

Two practical insights follow. First, a "normal" bicarbonate can hide two opposing processes (e.g. hyperchloraemic acidosis plus hypoalbuminaemic alkalosis) — Fencl et al. found that this was common in critically ill patients. Second, the acid–base effect of an intravenous fluid is predictable from its SID (see next section).

Where does 1 litre go? The principle

Water moves freely between the ICF and ECF to equalise osmolality. Sodium is largely confined to the ECF (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), and large proteins are largely confined to the plasma. So, in the classic idealised model:

  • Isotonic sodium-based crystalloids (0.9% saline, Hartmann's, Plasma-Lyte 148) stay in the ECF and distribute in proportion to plasma and interstitial volumes: roughly ¼ (≈ 250 mL) intravascular and ¾ interstitial at equilibrium. Hartmann's is mildly hypotonic, so a small fraction also enters cells.
  • 5% glucose: the glucose is metabolised, leaving free water that distributes through total body water: roughly ⅔ (≈ 670 mL) intracellular, ⅓ (≈ 330 mL) extracellular, of which only ≈ 1/12 (≈ 80 mL) remains intravascular. It is a source of water, not a resuscitation fluid.
  • Human albumin solution 4.5% is approximately iso-oncotic: in the idealised model it initially stays largely intravascular. In practice the benefit is smaller — in the SAFE trial the ratio of albumin to saline volume given was approximately 1:1.4, not the 1:4 the simple model predicts (Finfer et al. 2004).
  • Human albumin solution 20% is hyperoncotic: it draws interstitial water into the plasma, so the plasma volume expansion exceeds the volume infused (the magnitude depends on interstitial hydration and capillary integrity).

Use the numbers to understand principles, not to predict an individual patient's response.

The revised Starling principle and the glycocalyx (briefly)

  • The endothelial glycocalyx is the main semipermeable barrier. The oncotic gradient that opposes filtration is between plasma and the small space beneath the glycocalyx, not the bulk interstitial fluid (Levick & Michel 2010; Woodcock & Woodcock 2012).
  • In most tissues there is no sustained reabsorption at the venous end of capillaries in the steady state; filtered fluid returns to the circulation mainly via lymph.
  • Fluid handling is context-sensitive: when capillary pressure is low (e.g. hypovolaemia), infused crystalloid is retained in the circulation relatively well; when capillary pressure is normal or high, both crystalloid and colloid filter out more readily (Chappell et al. 2008; Woodcock & Woodcock 2012).
  • Inflammation, sepsis, surgery and hypervolaemia itself can damage the glycocalyx, increasing leak.

Why 0.9% saline causes a hyperchloraemic acidosis

0.9% saline contains equal concentrations of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ (154 mmol/L each), so its SID is zero. Infusing it moves the plasma SID towards zero: chloride rises proportionally more than sodium (plasma Cl⁻ ≈ 100 rises towards 154, while Na⁺ ≈ 140 barely changes). A fall in SID at a fixed PCO₂ raises [H⁺] and lowers [HCO₃⁻] — a hyperchloraemic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis, demonstrated in volunteers and surgical patients (Scheingraber et al. 1999).

The traditional explanation is "dilution of bicarbonate", but bicarbonate is a dependent variable; the Stewart explanation is that the infusion lowers SID.

What SID makes a fluid "balanced"? (Morgan's work)

Infusion also dilutes ATOT (albumin), which on its own is alkalinising. Morgan, Venkatesh and Hall (2002) showed in in-vitro haemodilution that a crystalloid with SID ≈ 24 mEq/L — about the normal plasma bicarbonate — leaves pH unchanged; a lower SID is acidifying and a higher SID alkalinising. Morgan and Venkatesh (2003) used this to discuss the design of "balanced" crystalloids. Hartmann's (effective SID ≈ 28) is close to neutral; Plasma-Lyte 148 (≈ 50, if acetate and gluconate are fully metabolised) is mildly alkalinising in large volumes; 0.9% saline (0) is acidifying.

Does it matter clinically?

Large randomised trials comparing balanced crystalloids with saline in critically ill adults have reported different results: SMART (Semler et al. 2018) found a small reduction in a composite of major adverse kidney events with balanced fluids, whereas PLUS (Finfer et al. 2022) found no difference in 90-day mortality or kidney outcomes. Follow your local and national guidance (in the UK, NICE guideline CG174 covers intravenous fluid therapy in adults in hospital).

Mechanism

  • In acute mineral (inorganic) acidosis, plasma K⁺ rises: on average about 0.6 mmol/L per 0.1 fall in pH, but with a very wide range (≈ 0.2–1.7) (Adrogué & Madias 1981).
  • The shift is mediated indirectly — a fall in extracellular pH and HCO₃⁻ reduces Na⁺/H⁺ exchange and Na⁺–HCO₃⁻ cotransport, lowering intracellular Na⁺ and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity — rather than by a simple one-for-one H⁺/K⁺ swap.
  • In organic acidoses (lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis) and in respiratory acidosis the shift is much smaller and inconsistent, because the organic anion or CO₂ can enter cells with the H⁺.
  • In DKA, hyperkalaemia on presentation is due mainly to insulin deficiency and hypertonicity, and it coexists with total-body potassium depletion; plasma K⁺ falls with insulin treatment.
  • Alkalaemia tends to lower plasma K⁺; the effect is generally smaller and variable.
